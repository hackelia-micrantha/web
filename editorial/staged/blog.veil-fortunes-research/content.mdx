A geometric image transformation and a UNIX fortune might seem like unrelated engineering problems. One changes how information looks. The other changes how an idea is expressed.

Yet [Veil](https://veil.micrantha.com) and [Fortunes](https://fortunes.micrantha.com) share an approach: start with an old, useful idea; study the limits of newer techniques; and resist confusing an impressive demonstration with a reliable result.

Here is what exists today, what we are investigating, and how specific papers help turn ideas into testable questions. These are **selected research references, not an academic citation ranking, implementation announcement, or claim that Micrantha has reproduced the authors' results**.

**Status as of November 2026:** Veil's existing image transformation is one-way. Fortunes' existing API serves an established collection of short UNIX-style humor and sayings; it does not use a generative model to answer each request. The systems described below are **research directions**, not deployed upgrades.

## Veil: change the image, preserve the possibility of recovery

**Today:** Veil renders source images as Voronoi-style geometric mosaics. Each cell uses a representative color, so information from the original image is discarded. That output is **one-way**; there is no encryption key that can reconstruct what the renderer threw away.

**Goal:** Obfuscate a visible image, encrypt the **original image file bytes**, and carry enough authenticated encrypted recovery information **within the obfuscated image artifact** to reconstruct the original using a separately retained key. The research is whether the artwork and recovery data can coexist in one usable, portable image—not whether a lossy Voronoi transformation can somehow be inverted.

The visible mosaic would remain readable in an ordinary image viewer. An authorized recovery process would need the intact carrier and the separate key; it would authenticate the stored recovery information before returning the original. None of that is a currently shipped image-recovery promise.

Those are different capabilities. An image that looks obscured is not necessarily anonymous; a deliberately recoverable image is **not irreversible redaction**. The system should make that distinction obvious rather than implying that visual distortion alone is a privacy guarantee.

Three questions guide the work:

- **Correctness:** Can restoration reproduce the original source exactly, rather than merely produce an image that looks similar? Image pixels, encoded file bytes, and metadata are different recovery targets.
- **Capacity and size:** How much information can be carried without making the resulting image impractical? Raw bit capacity is not the same as the size of an actual encoded, portable image.
- **Robustness and failure:** What survives a lossless round-trip? What breaks after cropping, resizing, JPEG recompression, or social-media processing? When recovery is impossible, failure should be explicit, not approximate or silent.

On-device processing, authenticated recovery information, and an independent key are important design constraints. The research does **not** establish that any ordinary image can survive arbitrary edits and still restore its original bytes.

### The proposed layers: where could the recovery information live?

Rather than simply attaching opaque data, the research asks whether the **visible obfuscation itself** can contribute to carrying the encrypted information. These are candidate *encoding surfaces*, not independently proven channels or an approved format:

1. **Cell geometry.** Controlled properties of a Voronoi layout may represent information while remaining a recognizable mosaic. Example question: can a recipient recover those choices deterministically from a lossless decoded image, without confusing incidental geometry with encoded bits?
2. **Cell boundaries and borders.** The edges already form a distinct visual structure. Their appearance might provide capacity, but anti-aliasing, re-encoding, and ambiguous border detection can corrupt recovery.
3. **Fine-scale color or texture.** Very small, keyed variations could carry bits inside regions where they are less visually distracting. These variations must be recoverable **exactly**, not merely look similar; authenticated encrypted bytes do not tolerate an occasional missing bit.
4. **Adaptive subdivision.** Some regions may support more detailed cells than others. Repeated or selective subdivision could allocate capacity to suitable areas, but describing the subdivisions and maintaining deterministic decoding also costs bits.
5. **Dedicated lossless capacity as a baseline.** Extra carrier pixels remain a simple comparison point if the visible layers cannot reliably carry enough information. They may make the image larger or visibly less elegant; simplicity and exactness still matter.

These layers could interact. A change to geometry may alter borders and texture simultaneously. **Adding theoretical bit capacities together would double-count information unless each channel can be decoded independently and its overhead measured.** Model-assisted selection of candidate regions is another hypothesis, not a prerequisite; a deterministic baseline comes first.

An illustrative research sequence would take a source image, produce the one-way Voronoi preview, encrypt the unmodified original, map the ciphertext into one or more *tested* image-carrier surfaces, and attempt recovery with a separate key. The critical acceptance test is **byte-for-byte equality of the recovered source**, not a visually faithful reconstruction.

### Six papers shaping Veil's questions

1. **[Learning Robust Image Watermarking with Lossless Cover Recovery](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/html/Chen_Learning_Robust_Image_Watermarking_with_Lossless_Cover_Recovery_ICCV_2025_paper.html)** — Chen et al., ICCV 2025. **How it helps:** separate two test cases—exact recovery with an intact carrier and watermark detection after image distortion. An illustration that restores the same decoded pixels but loses the original JPEG compression or metadata would **fail** Veil's exact-file target. The paper does not establish original-file byte recovery.

2. **[FlexMark: Adaptive Watermarking Method for Images](https://www.cs.sfu.ca/~mhefeeda/Papers/mmsys24_flexmark.pdf)** — Arab, Ghorbanpour and Hefeeda, ACM MMSys 2024. **How it helps:** compare uniform cell placement against selectively allocated carrier capacity. For example, a smooth region and a highly detailed region may tolerate different visual modifications; the experiment should still score *recoverable* bits, output size, and decoding cost, not appearance alone. Its neural approach is a research comparator, not a decision to deploy a large model.

3. **[We Can Hide More Bits: The Unused Watermarking Capacity in Theory and in Practice](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12812)** — Petrov et al., 2025 preprint. **How it helps:** distinguish apparent writable capacity from authenticated, zero-error payload capacity. For example, a carrier that recovers 99% of embedded bits may be useful for a watermark but unusable for a complete encrypted original. Count framing, redundancy, and side information before claiming net capacity.

4. **[A Reversible Data Hiding Method in Encrypted Images for Controlling Trade-Off between Hiding Capacity and Compression Efficiency](https://www.mdpi.com/2313-433X/7/12/268)** — Motomura, Imaizumi and Kiya, 2021. **How it helps:** compare embedding choices by **final lossless file size**, not raw bits per pixel. A densely coded border or texture layer may look economical in raster memory while increasing encoded size substantially. Include the original-file bytes, ciphertext overhead, and all allocation metadata in the comparison.

5. **[High-Capacity Reversible Data Hiding in Encrypted Images Based on Pixel Prediction and QuadTree Decomposition](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/13/23/12706)** — Alqahtani and Masmoudi, 2023. **How it helps:** test whether selectively subdividing some mosaic regions improves *net* recovery capacity, after accounting for the partition description and associated data. More subdivisions are not automatically better: they may make the image busier, enlarge metadata, or make decoding fragile. The paper does not prove an optimal Veil layout or original-file recovery.

6. **[A Feature-based Fragile Watermarking for Tamper Detection using Voronoi Diagram Decomposition](https://aircconline.com/csit/papers/vol10/csit101012.pdf)** — Golea and Melkemi, 2020. **How it helps:** reason about region-addressed information and the possibility of locating a damaged area. For example, a changed cell border could be detected as an integrity failure; it should **not** trigger speculative reconstruction of missing original bytes. Fragile watermark checks and region repair are not substitutes for authenticated, exact restoration.

**What we would measure:** exact source-file hashes after recovery, net recoverable encrypted bytes per final image byte, image legibility, decode cost, peak memory, and negative cases such as cropping, color conversion, and JPEG recompression. Compare a simple lossless carrier with at most a small number of layered candidates on the same images. If any layer fails to preserve complete authenticated data, it contributes **zero** usable exact-recovery capacity in that configuration.

The simplest proven arrangement should win. A new encoding technique or AI model has to justify its added complexity with measured benefit.

## Fortunes: carry UNIX folklore into the present

**Today:** Fortunes is a deliberately straightforward service and API for retrieving curated, often sarcastic UNIX-style humor, sayings, and quotations. It is not an AI joke generator. Its simplicity is a feature: short text, predictable delivery, and a collection worth revisiting.

The UNIX `fortune` tradition is more than a random quotation command. Its appeal is the unexpected collision of a compact sentence with the reader's day: a joke, a contradiction, an aphorism, a technical quirk, or an observation that becomes funnier because it arrives without warning.

Fortunes aims to **continue that tradition into modern life and technology**. Its subject matter includes programming languages, terminals, operating systems, development tools, infrastructure, software culture, and wider social behavior. The quirks of real tools matter as much as abstract commentary about computing.

There are **two complementary research paths**, not a requirement to combine several quotations every time:

- **Tool-native humor:** Find a funny double meaning, literal error message, naming collision, surprising state, or contradiction **within one real tool or command**. The old `man woman` gag is a historical example of a manual-page lookup being read as a statement in ordinary English. Git's `detached HEAD` state offers another kind of technical ambiguity. The experiment is whether AI notices *why* the language is funny, while remaining accurate about the tool's behavior.
- **Multi-concept irony:** Combine two or three compatible observations—perhaps a Git feature, a workplace habit, and an expectation about accountability—to expose a relation that was not obvious in any one premise. A genuine conceptual bridge matters more than keyword overlap.

The first can be a short pun with one fact; the second might be a more elaborate satirical insight. Both must be judged by people who understand the reference. Recognizing a pattern in a command name is not the same as proving a joke is original, funny, or appropriate.

**Goal:** Continue that sarcastic UNIX folklore into the modern era through an **AI-enabled fortune composition and curation pipeline**. It could find amusing language **inside technology itself**, identify contradictions between how tools work and how people talk, combine two or three observations **when useful**, and propose succinct fortunes for human review. A one-premise technical pun must remain a first-class option.

The real research question is **whether an AI system can recognize the incongruity, irony, timing, and shared assumptions that make people find something funny**, rather than merely remix the words of existing jokes. Relevance, fluency, and lexical novelty are weaker goals than a coherent new joke.

The existing corpus and human-written material remain central. Offline or batch drafting is the preferred research path; the public API need not—and currently does not—call a model for each request.

### From observations to an original fortune

A proposed **editorial pipeline**, not a new set of microservices or models, separates the operations that are easy to conflate:

```text
Curated observations and verified tool behavior
  -> identify small factual idea seeds and provenance
  -> categorize subject, audience, humor mechanism, rights
  -> select one, two, or three complementary concepts
  -> identify a real relationship or contradiction
  -> try irony, inversion, misdirection, or wordplay
  -> draft compact original wording
  -> verify tool facts, novelty, rights, and suitability
  -> human editorial approval
  -> separately governed corpus publication
  -> existing predictable API reads approved entries
```

A useful editorial sequence looks like this:

1. Collect small, source-checked observations or premises about a tool, technology, or social behavior.
2. Label the subject, underlying contradiction, tone, and intended audience.
3. Select one strong premise or a complementary pair or trio; identify an actual conceptual bridge, not just related keywords.
4. Draft a short *new expression* and evaluate whether the surprising conclusion is also coherent and technically true.
5. Check for near-copying, attribution and rights issues, needless harm, repetition, and weak humor; let a human decide what joins the published corpus.

A pair of existing quotations does not become original simply because a model combines them. Likewise, a statement can be relevant, grammatically fluent, and novel without being funny.

### Worked examples: technical wordplay and conceptual irony

Git is a useful research subject because its real commands carry both technical meaning and a vocabulary that overlaps with human behavior. The modern candidate lines below are **illustrative, unverified drafts for the article**, not existing Fortunes entries, model results, or cleared-for-publication jokes. The older UNIX example is labeled separately because it is folklore rather than a new invention.

**Example A — the classic `man woman` command (historical reference)**

- **Tool behavior:** `man` searches manual pages. On systems without a manual page named `woman`, `man woman` may produce an error such as "No manual entry for woman"; output depends on the installed manuals and implementation. See this [historical UNIX humor example](https://www.linux.co.cr/lg/issue86/TWDT.html).
- **Mechanism:** The word `man` doubles as a manual-page command and an English noun. A literal missing-document error is read as a human statement.
- **Research question:** Can a model detect command-name ambiguity and unintended double meanings in modern tools **without copying** the old line or mistaking a contingent message for a universal command result?
- **Editorial caution:** The historical punchline can also trade on a gender stereotype. Its inclusion here illustrates *how* technical wordplay works, not an endorsement or a candidate for a modern corpus.

**Example B — Git detached HEAD (new illustrative draft)**

- **Verified tool fact:** In [Git's detached HEAD state](https://git-scm.com/docs/user-manual#detached-head), `HEAD` refers directly to a commit instead of a branch. You can still create commits, but a new branch or another reference is needed if you want to keep that line of work readily reachable after switching away.
- **Mechanism:** A technical name invokes ordinary-language ideas of identity, direction, and commitment.
- **Candidate wording:** "Git says my HEAD is detached. I'm still making commits; I just don't belong to a branch."
- **Review question:** Is the double meaning funny to a developer, or merely an accurate description with a human metaphor attached? Does the phrasing already exist elsewhere?

The contrast matters: the `man` and detached-HEAD examples start with **one tool-native ambiguity**. The next two start with **relations between multiple ideas**, where humor depends on discovering a bridge rather than just noticing a double meaning.

**Example C — accountability versus root cause**

- **Verified tool fact:** [`git blame`](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-blame) identifies the revision and author that last changed each line; it does not prove why a bug happened.
- **Second concept:** An organization can mistake identifying a person for explaining a systemic failure.
- **Candidate bridge:** *blame is attribution, not causality*.
- **Draft fortune:** "Git can tell us who last edited the line. For why it broke, we scheduled a meeting."
- **Review question:** Is that an actual irony or just a familiar complaint about meetings? Does a Git-literate reader find it funny?

**Example D — cleaner history versus persistent memory**

- **Verified tool facts:** [`git rebase`](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-rebase) reapplies commits onto a different base. A local [`git reflog`](https://git-scm.com/docs/git-reflog) records updates to references, subject to local configuration and retention.
- **Third concept:** Teams sometimes polish the story of how a decision was made.
- **Candidate bridge:** *a cleaner public narrative can coexist with a less tidy local history*.
- **Draft fortune:** "We rebased the story for a cleaner history. The reflog remembered the edits."
- **Review question:** Does the metaphor reveal a new observation, or merely map Git command names onto a cliché? No claim is made that Git always preserves every earlier version.

The challenge for AI is not just to see that `rebase` and `reflog` are Git commands, or that `man` and `HEAD` have meanings outside a terminal. It is to recognize **when one command is funny on its own versus when a new relationship between ideas creates the irony**, determine whether the inference is true enough to support a joke, and express it without turning into a paragraph of explanation. It should be allowed to reject every candidate.

### Six papers shaping Fortunes' questions

1. **[Grounded Satirical Generation with RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10853)** — Itkonen et al., 2026. **How it helps:** give a drafting system verified information about a command or modern practice, then ask whether its satire is actually funny. A technically faithful `git blame` fact is only a premise; grounding cannot supply the punchline on its own. The reported relevance improvement did not clearly establish better humor.

2. **[A Computational Framework for Conceptual Blending](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S000437021730142X)** — Eppe et al., 2018. **How it helps:** explicitly describe the shared structural relationship between two or three concepts. In the Git example, the relevant bridge is attribution versus explanation, not the accidental co-occurrence of "Git" and "management." A coherent blend is not automatically a good joke.

3. **[PopBlends: Strategies for Conceptual Blending with Large Language Models](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580948)** — Wang et al., CHI 2023. **How it helps:** a curator can ask for several plausible connections between developer practices and everyday human contradictions, then choose or reject them. This suggests divergent *idea exploration* followed by convergence; it does not prove that more generations create better fortunes.

4. **[Humor Mechanics: Advancing Humor Generation with Multistep Reasoning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.07280)** — Tikhonov and Shtykovskiy, ICCC 2024. **How it helps:** distinguish finding associations, recognizing a comic mechanism, and writing the final line—whether starting with one command-name pun such as `detached HEAD` or several observations requiring a bridge. The authors report less evaluator-recognized familiarity in one multistep condition, but funniness evidence is less conclusive; adding stages or prompts is not a guaranteed improvement over direct generation.

5. **[What Makes an Ideal Quote? Recommending "Unexpected yet Rational" Quotations via Novelty](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.362/)** — Chang et al., ACL 2026. **How it helps:** review two separate properties: was the association unexpected, and does it make sense after the surprise? The work measures selection of existing quotes, not new joke invention, so its ranking results should not become an automatic humor score.

6. **[Engagement Undermines Safety: How Stereotypes and Toxicity Shape Humor in Language Models](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.373/)** — Dogra et al., EACL 2026. **How it helps:** keep quality selection and content eligibility separate. Models and engagement-based judges can reward cheap stereotypes or shock, so any clever Git or social joke still needs human review for target, accuracy, and rights. The paper does not imply that satire must avoid criticism or irreverence.

**What we would measure:** a small, offline, blinded comparison using a fixed set of source-checked technical and social premises, including real **command names, diagnostic messages, and version-specific tool states**. Compare direct prompting with fact-grounded and explicitly contradiction-guided drafting; let reviewers independently score humor, coherent surprise, technical fidelity, near-copy risk, and suitability. Record whether a joke works through **one tool-native double meaning** or a **multi-premise conceptual bridge**, without creating new required pipeline stages. The idea of selecting two or three complementary seeds is a **separate hypothesis** to test only if the simpler baseline demonstrates a meaningful gap. No experiments or product changes are claimed here.

More generated text, model steps, or rankings are not themselves progress; the relevant output is a small number of original, accurate, genuinely amusing **human-approved** fortunes.

## What the two projects have in common

Veil starts with a **working one-way image transformation** and asks whether encrypted original-file recovery can be embedded in the artwork. Fortunes starts with a **working curated UNIX-humor API** and asks whether human-guided AI can discover, compose, and evaluate new irony about today's tools and society.

Both expose the limits of attractive proxy metrics:

- Veil cannot substitute visual similarity, advertised embedding capacity, or average bit accuracy for exact recovery.
- Fortunes cannot substitute retrieval relevance, model-rated funniness, or output volume for human judgment.
- Neither project should treat a paper's reported measurements as independently reproduced Micrantha results.

The near-term objective is modest: better-defined experiments, smaller candidate sets, explicit failure cases, and evidence that can support a future product decision.

A successful result might justify more development. An unsuccessful result may justify a simpler approach—or stopping. That is part of the research too.
